The Concept

What is a Death Spiral?

When demand drops, fixed costs must be spread over fewer units — raising the cost per unit, which can trigger further demand loss. Peloton is a textbook case.

Historical Context

How Peloton Fell Into the Spiral

The death spiral didn't happen overnight. Here's the sequence of events.

FY20
FY2020 · Revenue: $1.83B

🚀 Pandemic Boom

COVID lockdowns drive explosive demand. Revenue nearly doubles. Peloton can't manufacture bikes fast enough. Fixed costs easily absorbed across a surging subscriber base.

FY21
FY2021 · Revenue: $4.02B · Peak

📈 All-Time High

Revenue hits $4B. Peloton invests aggressively — $337M in CapEx, $248M in R&D, $662M in SG&A. Fixed cost base balloons. This bet requires sustained demand growth.

FY22
FY2022 · Revenue: $3.58B · Net Loss: −$2.83B

💥 The Collapse Begins

Gyms reopen. Demand evaporates. Revenue falls 11%. But fixed costs — built for a $4B company — don't shrink. Cost of revenue alone is $2.88B on $3.58B in sales. Gross margin collapses to 19.5%.

FY23
FY2023 · Revenue: $2.80B · Net Loss: −$1.26B

🔻 Death Spiral Deepens

Revenue falls another 22%. Restructuring charges of $189M. Churn rises to 1.3%. Each subscriber now carries a heavier fixed cost burden. The spiral is self-reinforcing.

FY24
FY2024 · Revenue: $2.70B · Net Loss: −$552M

🔧 Restructuring Begins

New CEO Barry McCarthy implements cost cuts. Gross margin recovers to 44.7%. But subscriber churn rises to 1.4% and paid subs fall to ~2.98M. FCF still negative at −$86M.

FY25
FY2025 · Revenue: $2.49B · Net Loss: −$119M

⚖️ Stabilizing, But Not Fixed

FCF turns positive at +$324M. Subscription gross margin hits 69.1%. But churn rises to 1.6%, subs fall to ~2.77M, and $1.5B in debt at 10.9% interest keeps fixed costs elevated. The spiral is slowing — not reversed.

CFO Insight

What This Means for Peloton's CFO

The Core Problem: Fixed Costs Built for a Bigger Business

Peloton built its cost structure for a $4B company. Today it operates a $2.5B company — but the fixed cost base (debt service, platform infrastructure, content production) doesn't scale down proportionally. Every subscriber who churns forces the remaining ~2.77M members to absorb a larger share of those fixed costs, which weakens the value proposition and accelerates further churn. This is the textbook demand-driven death spiral from cost allocation economics.

The Practical Capacity Problem

Peloton's content platform, instructor talent, and technology infrastructure were built for theoretical capacity far above current utilization. Using a practical capacity overhead rate — allocating fixed costs based on what the platform can handle, not what it currently serves — would reveal the true cost of excess capacity: roughly $134.5M in annual interest alone divided across 2.77M subscribers equals ~$48.5 per subscriber per year in debt service alone, before any other fixed costs. That's a structural drag that churn makes worse.

The Path Out: Subscription Revenue as the Lifeline

The 69.1% subscription gross margin is Peloton's most valuable asset. At $44/month per subscriber, the subscription business generates strong incremental contribution margin. The CFO's challenge is to stabilize and grow this base — even modestly — while continuing to reduce fixed overhead. If churn can be brought back to 1.2% (Q3 FY25 levels) and held, the math begins to work. The spiral can be broken, but only if fixed cost reduction outpaces subscriber attrition.
